# Source text
Section 28. (...) (4) No law granting any tax exemption shall be passed without the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of the Congress.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- A law granting a tax exemption needs the concurrence of a majority of ALL the Members of Congress, not just a majority of those present (Art. VI, Sec. 28(4)).
- Taxation is the rule and exemption the exception, so tax exemptions are construed strictissimi juris (strictly) against the taxpayer.
- The taxpayer claiming an exemption must point to a clear grant in the law; an exemption is never presumed or extended by implication.
- In contrast, a doubtful law IMPOSING a tax is construed against the government and in favour of the taxpayer.

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: A bill granting a five-year tax exemption to small rice mills was approved in each House by a majority of the members present, which was less than a majority of all the members. Was a valid exemption granted?
Model answer: No. Under Art. VI, Sec. 28(4), no law granting any tax exemption may be passed without the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of Congress. A majority of only those present is not enough, so the exemption was not validly granted.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem.) Assume a law grants non-stock, non-profit hospitals an exemption from income tax "on income derived from hospital services." Buhay Hospital, a non-stock, non-profit hospital, also leases space in its building to a coffee shop and a bank, earning P5 million in rent. When the BIR assessed income tax on the rent, the hospital argued that the rent supports its hospital work, so it should be covered by the exemption. Is the rental income exempt? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: No. The rental income is not exempt from income tax.
- Legal basis: A tax exemption must be clearly granted by law, and laws granting exemptions are construed strictissimi juris against the taxpayer; an exemption is never extended by implication. The constitutional rule that an exemption needs the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of Congress (Art. VI, Sec. 28(4)) shows that exemptions are exceptional and must come from the law itself.
- Application: The law exempts only income "derived from hospital services." Rent from leasing space to a coffee shop and a bank is not income from hospital services. The hospital's argument that the rent supports its work asks the court to extend the exemption by implication, which strict construction does not allow. The hospital bears the burden of showing a clear grant, and the law grants none for rent.
- Conclusion: Read strictly against the taxpayer, the exemption covers only hospital-service income, so the P5 million rent is taxable.
