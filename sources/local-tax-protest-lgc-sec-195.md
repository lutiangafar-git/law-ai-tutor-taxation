# Source text
Section 195. Protest of Assessment. - When the local treasurer or his duly authorized representative finds that correct taxes, fees, or charges have not been paid, he shall issue a notice of assessment stating the nature of the tax, fee, or charge, the amount of deficiency, the surcharges, interests and penalties. Within sixty (60) days from the receipt of the notice of assessment, the taxpayer may file a written protest with the local treasurer contesting the assessment; otherwise, the assessment shall become final and executory. The local treasurer shall decide the protest within sixty (60) days from the time of its filing. If the local treasurer finds the protest to be wholly or partly meritorious, he shall issue a notice cancelling wholly or partially the assessment. However, if the local treasurer finds the assessment to be wholly or partly correct, he shall deny the protest wholly or partly with notice to the taxpayer. The taxpayer shall have thirty (30) days from the receipt of the denial of the protest or from the lapse of the sixty (60) day period prescribed herein within which to appeal with the court of competent jurisdiction otherwise the assessment becomes conclusive and unappealable.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- The taxpayer has 60 days from receipt of the notice of assessment to file a written protest with the local treasurer; otherwise the assessment becomes final and executory (Sec. 195).
- The local treasurer must decide the protest within 60 days from its filing (Sec. 195).
- The taxpayer then has 30 days from receipt of the denial, or from the lapse of the treasurer's 60-day period, to appeal to the court of competent jurisdiction (Sec. 195).
- If the taxpayer misses the 30 days, the assessment becomes conclusive and unappealable (Sec. 195).
- So when the treasurer does not act, the 30 days run from the end of the 60-day period, not from some later decision.

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: Kusina Corp. received a local business tax assessment on 2 March. It filed its written protest with the city treasurer on 20 May. Was the protest on time?
Model answer: No. Under Sec. 195, a written protest must be filed within 60 days from receipt of the notice of assessment. From 2 March, 60 days ended on 1 May. The protest filed on 20 May is late, so the assessment became final and executory.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem. Related issues were asked in the 2010 and 2014 Bar Examinations, Taxation Law.) Tindahan Inc. received a notice of local business tax assessment from the city treasurer and filed a written protest 20 days later. The treasurer never acted on the protest. Tindahan waited, hoping for a decision, and filed an appeal in court 120 days after it had filed the protest. The city argues that the appeal is too late. Is the city correct? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: Yes. Tindahan's appeal was filed too late, and the assessment has become conclusive and unappealable.
- Legal basis: Under Sec. 195 of the Local Government Code, the local treasurer must decide a protest within 60 days from its filing. The taxpayer has 30 days from receipt of the denial or from the lapse of that 60-day period within which to appeal to the court of competent jurisdiction; otherwise, the assessment becomes conclusive and unappealable.
- Application: Tindahan's protest, filed 20 days after receipt, was on time. But the treasurer did not decide it, so the treasurer's 60-day period lapsed 60 days after the protest was filed. From then, Tindahan had 30 days to appeal, that is, until 90 days after filing the protest. Its appeal came 120 days after the protest, 30 days too late. Waiting for a decision does not stop the 30-day period, which runs from the lapse of the 60 days.
- Conclusion: Because the appeal was filed beyond the 30 days counted from the lapse of the treasurer's 60-day period, the assessment became conclusive and unappealable, and the city is correct.
